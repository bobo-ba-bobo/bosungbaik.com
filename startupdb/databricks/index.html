Databricks

San Francisco / Data & AI / Founded 2013 / Private
Databricks sells the place where a company keeps all of its data and does all of its computing on top of it - reporting, dashboards, machine learning, and now AI agents. The pitch is that those used to be two separate purchases, a data lake for cheap raw storage and a data warehouse for clean queryable tables, and that you should only have to buy one. They called the merged thing a lakehouse, and spent a decade making the word mean something.

What it actually does

Start with the problem. A large company generates data in a hundred places - transactions, app logs, sensors, support tickets, documents, video. Historically it went to two homes. A data warehouse (Teradata, later Snowflake) held clean, structured tables: fast, governed, expensive, and useless for anything that isn't a row and a column. A data lake (Hadoop, later S3) held everything else as raw files: cheap and flexible, but with no transactions, no guarantees, and no real governance. Analysts lived in the warehouse, data scientists lived in the lake, and companies paid to copy data back and forth between them forever.

Databricks' answer is to leave the data in cheap object storage in open file formats, and then add the parts that made a warehouse worth paying for - transactions, schema, permissions, fast SQL - as a layer on top. One copy of the data, one permission model, and every workload pointed at it. That is the lakehouse.

The pieces

  • Apache Spark - the distributed compute engine the founders wrote before the company existed. Still the engine underneath a lot of it.
  • Delta Lake - a transaction log written alongside Parquet files. This is the trick that gives a pile of files in S3 the behaviour of a database table.
  • Unity Catalog - one permission and lineage system covering tables, files, models, and dashboards. The governance layer is what makes a large enterprise willing to consolidate.
  • Databricks SQL and Photon - a warehouse-grade SQL surface with a C++ vectorized engine, built to take BI workloads off Snowflake.
  • MLflow and Mosaic AI - experiment tracking, model serving, fine-tuning and agent tooling. The AI half of the product.
  • Lakebase - serverless Postgres, bought in via Neon. Operational database sitting next to the analytical one, aimed at applications and agents.

How it makes money

Consumption, not seats. Customers buy compute measured in DBUs, and the software runs inside the customer's own AWS, Azure, or GCP account rather than on Databricks infrastructure. That has two consequences worth noticing: the cloud vendors are distribution partners rather than pure enemies (Azure Databricks is a first-party Microsoft service), and revenue expands automatically as a customer moves more workloads onto the platform. Land with data engineering, expand into BI, then ML, then AI.

History

2009
Matei Zaharia starts Spark as a research project at UC Berkeley's AMPLab, as a faster answer to Hadoop MapReduce. Open-sourced the following year.
2013
Seven people out of AMPLab found Databricks - Ali Ghodsi, Matei Zaharia, Ion Stoica, Reynold Xin, Patrick Wendell, Andy Konwinski, Arsalan Tavakoli-Shiraji. Spark is donated to the Apache Software Foundation the same year, and Andreessen Horowitz puts in $14M. Giving away the engine and selling the platform is the strategy from day one.
2015
The commercial product ships on AWS: hosted Spark clusters plus collaborative notebooks. For years this is essentially "managed Spark".
2016
Ali Ghodsi takes over as CEO from Ion Stoica, who returns to Berkeley.
2017
Azure Databricks launches as a first-party Microsoft service, sold and billed by Microsoft. An unusually good distribution deal, and a large part of why the company scaled in the enterprise as fast as it did.
2018
MLflow released as open source. Delta Lake introduced.
2019
Delta Lake is open-sourced under the Linux Foundation. Two rounds in one year take the valuation from $2.75B to $6.2B.
2020
The company publishes the lakehouse argument and starts naming the category rather than describing a product. Redash is acquired to fill in dashboards, and SQL Analytics launches - the first direct move onto warehouse territory.
2021
$1B at $28B in February, $1.6B at $38B in August. Photon ships, Unity Catalog is announced, GCP support lands. Snowflake, public since 2020, becomes the reference competitor for both companies' pitches.
2022
Crosses $1B in annualized revenue.
2023
ChatGPT resets the category. Databricks buys MosaicML for about $1.3B to own model training and serving rather than rent it, and raises $500M at $43B.
2024
Releases DBRX, an open model, as proof the AI story is real. Acquires Tabular, the company founded by the creators of Apache Iceberg - the rival table format - which effectively ends the format war by buying both sides. Closes a $10B Series J at $62B in December, one of the largest venture rounds ever raised.
2025
Buys Neon for about $1B and turns it into Lakebase, moving from analytics into the operational database that applications and agents actually write to. Tecton follows for around $900M. Series K in September raises $1B at over $100B, and a $5B round at $134B is announced in December.
2026
The $134B round closes in February. In July a strategic round led by Coatue marks the company at $188B, closing in August near $190B with revenue run-rate at $7B growing more than 80% year over year. Ghodsi calls 2026 a bad year to go public and points at 2027 at the earliest.

Competition

  • Snowflake - the mirror image. Snowflake started as a warehouse and grew down into data science and open formats; Databricks started as an engine over a lake and grew up into SQL and governance. They now sell against each other in nearly every enterprise deal.
  • The cloud vendors - Microsoft Fabric, Google BigQuery, and AWS all ship competing first-party stacks while also hosting and reselling Databricks. Partner and competitor at the same time.
  • The AI labs - as buyers shift budget from "store and query our data" to "build agents on our data", OpenAI and Anthropic sit adjacent to part of the same wallet.

Why I keep an eye on it

Four things I think are transferable to earlier-stage companies.

  • Open source as distribution, not as product. Spark, Delta Lake, and MLflow were given away and became defaults. The company never sold them. It sold the operational burden of running them, which is the part enterprises will actually pay for.
  • Naming the category is a competitive act. "Lakehouse" was a positioning decision before it was a working product, and it forced every competitor to argue on Databricks' terms.
  • M&A to buy standards. Tabular is the sharpest example: rather than winning the Delta versus Iceberg format war, they bought the other side's founders and made the fight irrelevant. Neon and Electric are the same move aimed at the agent era.
  • Staying private is now a strategy. Thirteen years old, $7B run-rate, thirteen priced rounds, still no S-1. Private capital is deep enough that an IPO has become optional, which changes how a seed investor should think about the length of the hold.

The open question is whether the lakehouse remains the centre of gravity. Databricks is betting that agents need governed access to enterprise data, so whoever owns the data plane owns the agent layer too. The counter-case is that the model providers push down into the data plane themselves. The Neon, Tecton, and Electric purchases are the company placing that bet with cash.
